# 42-00-00 — Data System

## 1. General

The data system moves status and command traffic between the ship's
controllers and the equipment distributed through the hull. It is organized
as a command/response data bus: **DB-A**. One **bus controller (BC)** talks;
the **remote terminals (RTs)** answer. No terminal speaks unless the
controller asks, and the controller asks on a fixed cyclic schedule.

Like every load on the vessel, data hardware traces to a named breaker:
the bus controller (P/N 42-100-001) on CB E2, RT 5 on CB E3, RT 12 on
CB A2 (P/N 42-110-001). There are no exceptions for avionics.

## 2. The harness: trunk, couplers, stubs

The bus is a physical thing before it is a logical one, and most of its
failures are physical. A twinax trunk runs from the controller through
**bus couplers** (P/N 42-120-001) — the network's junction boxes, each
feeding the trunk through and tapping **stubs** to the terminals. On this
vessel: BC — seg.bc-j1 — coupler J1 — seg.j1-j2 — coupler J2, with RT 5
stubbed at J1 and RT 12 at J2 (WDM 42 has the drawing).

Rules the harness lives by:

- **Both trunk ends are terminated** at the cable's nominal 78 ohm. The
  terminators are part of the wiring, not of any box — and they are what a
  DMM reads on a healthy, de-energized trunk.
- **Stubs are transformer-coupled.** A fault on a stub or in its terminal —
  a short, a break, a dead module — is contained to that tap. One module
  cannot take the bus down through its stub; that is what the coupling
  transformer is *for*.
- **The trunk has no such mercy.** A short on the trunk or in a coupler
  kills the whole medium. A break partitions it: everything beyond the
  break goes silent from the controller's chair, and the dark terminals
  are always contiguous from the far end.
- **A coupler is a component, not a promise.** A failed coupler behaves
  like a break at that point, and it is a replaceable part, not a mystery.
  It is, however, the one part that cannot testify about itself: an open
  feed-through splits the coupler into two faces, each reading its own way
  out as though nothing were wrong, so a coupler break is confirmed from
  the *next coupler down*. A shorted coupler is the reverse — it sits
  across the pair at its own probe point and reads ~0 in every trunk
  direction from there. FIM 42-12 §3 works both.

A babbling transmitter (a terminal whose transceiver has latched
*stuck-dominant*) is the one failure that ignores all of this: it jams
the medium logically, from anywhere, coupling or no coupling. See §4.

## 3. The schedule, and how failures are declared

The controller polls every terminal on its schedule once per cycle (0.1 s).
A terminal that is powered, healthy, and *reachable* — an unbroken harness
path from the controller — answers every poll. The controller keeps the
score, and the score is all it keeps: it knows that a terminal answered or
did not, never *why*.

- A missed answer counts as one error against that terminal.
- Three consecutive misses and the controller declares the terminal
  **FAILED**; the bus is then **DEGRADED**.
- The first good answer clears the declaration immediately.
- The error counter never rewinds. A terminal that failed and recovered
  still shows its misses — that is the evidence trail, and it is normal.

## 4. DATA BUS A DEGRADED

The annunciator means exactly this: *the controller has declared at least
one terminal FAILED.* It does not mean the bus wire is damaged, a terminal
is dead, a feed breaker is open, or a transmitter is babbling — the
controller cannot distinguish any of those, and neither can you from the
annunciator alone. That distinction is what the bus table (§5) and the
FIM are for.

The table splits the world in two, and the split is the whole game:

- **One terminal dark, the rest answering:** the suspect is local to that
  terminal — its feed, its stub, its board. (FIM 42-12.)
- **Every terminal dark at once:** the medium or the controller. Either a
  babbling terminal is jamming the bus (FIM 42-11 — power-cycling finds
  it), or the trunk itself is shorted or broken (FIM 42-12 — the DMM
  finds it).

The bus table cannot tell you the root cause. It tells you which pattern
you are in — isolation begins there, not ends there.

## 5. The bus table (analyzer)

`data.db.a read` shows the controller's own report: bus state, and per
terminal its answer state and lifetime error count. This is the bus
analyzer in its simple form. Treat it as an instrument reading, because
that is what it is: it is the controller's view, subject to the controller
being alive to have one.

The couplers add the second instrument: a DMM across the pair at J1 or J2
(`data.db.a.j1 read`, `data.db.a.j2 read`), one reading per direction.
On a healthy de-energized trunk you read the 78 ohm terminators; an open
reads OL, a short reads ~0, and a stub tap reads its coupling-winding
continuity (~2 ohm). **De-energize the bus before ohms checks** — the
meter refuses a live bus, and that refusal is correct (FIM 42-12 teaches
the sequence).

Two readings can look alike and mean different parts: a short in a run and
a short in the coupler at that run's end both put ~0 at both ends of the
run. The meter has told you all it can, and the rest is done by
replacement in cost order (FIM 42-12 §3 step 5). Replacing hardware tells
you nothing by itself either — the bus table after re-energizing is the
only verdict there is.

## 6. Silence is not health

The controller is itself an instrument, and it obeys the same law as every
other instrument on the ship: **unpowered means silent.** If CB E2 is open
or BUS E is lost, the bus table reads NO DATA and the DEGRADED annunciator
cannot fire — not because the bus is healthy, but because nothing is
watching it. When the data system is suspect, verify the controller's own
feed first (CB E2, BUS E voltage), then the bus table, then the terminals.

A dead bus with a dead controller is indistinguishable from a healthy bus
until the controller is back on line. There is no warning for the absence
of a warning.

## 7. Power-up order (NOTE)

Energize terminal feeds before the controller feed. A controller that
comes on line before its terminals will, correctly, declare them FAILED —
the DEGRADED annunciator will raise and then clear as the terminals answer.
This is expected behavior, not a fault; the error counters will show it
happened for the rest of the flight. Crews that mind their records energize
the terminals first.

## 8. What the bus carries, and the age of a reading

Not every instrument on this ship is wired to its gauge. Two of them sit out
at their loads and report through the terminal in their bay:

| Reading | What it measures | Carried by | Behind it |
|---|---|---|---|
| `eps.load.cabin` | cabin load current | RT 12 | CB A2, BUS A |
| `eps.load.avionics` | avionics load current | RT 5 | CB E3, BUS E |

Everything else — bus voltages, battery volts and state of charge, breaker
and contactor positions — is **panel-wired**: copper from the sensor to the
indication, no network in between. That is deliberate and it is not an
accident of fitting. SOM 24-30-01 step 1 asks you to read BUS E with the ship
cold and dark; an indication that needed the data bus could not be read until
the data bus was powered, and the procedure that brings the ship up would have
nothing to bring it up with. The instruments you start the ship with do not
depend on the ship being started.

**A carried reading is only as live as its terminal.** The sensor keeps
sensing whatever the bus is doing — nothing out there stops measuring — but if
its terminal is not answering the controller, nothing it measures reaches you.
The indication then holds the last value that got through, and it **ages**.

Every reading you take carries its age. Past one second the indication is
marked stale — `?` on the panel, dimmed; `? STALE` in the printed line:

```
> eps.load.cabin read
mt.load.cabin.i:    5.357 A    src: mt.load.cabin.i (24-150-002)  age 4.3 s  ? STALE
```

The number is not a lie. It is the last honest reading that terminal
delivered, and it may still be perfectly correct — which is exactly the
trap. A frozen indication does not announce itself by being wrong. It
announces itself by not changing, and by the age climbing beside it. Read the
age with the number, always; that is what it is printed for.

**Stale is not zero, and stale is not healthy.** A reading that has aged out
tells you nothing about the quantity it names — the cabin may be drawing
exactly what it was, or nothing at all, and this indication cannot tell the
difference. Neither can the caution system: a monitor watching a stale source
goes quiet and drops any caution it was holding, for the same reason the
controller goes quiet when it loses power (§6). **A dark annunciator whose
source is stale is a statement about the instrument, not a clearance for the
system.**

So a frozen indication is a fault in the *reporting chain* until proven
otherwise, and the proof is cheap: the panel-wired instruments cannot go
stale. Bus voltage normal and the load's breaker closed means the load is
being fed, whatever its ammeter has stopped saying. FIM 42-14 works the
discrimination in order.

## 9. The particle environment

The ship flies through a flux of charged particles, and that flux is what
eventually flips a bit in an avionics board. Two sources, and they behave
completely differently:

- **Galactic background.** Always present, effectively constant, very low.
  This is the 0.1 p/cm²·s the monitor reads on a quiet day. Boards fail at
  this level too — a terminal will latch up eventually, on a timescale of
  months. There is nothing to do about it and nothing to watch for.
- **Solar particle events.** A flare or a coronal ejection raises the flux by
  three to five orders of magnitude over minutes to hours, then decays over
  hours to days. During the peak, the board failure rate rises by the same
  factor. This is not a different kind of hazard; it is the same hazard,
  turned up until it becomes a scheduling problem.

**The monitor.** `data.seu read` is an SEU environment monitor (P/N
42-160-001) in the avionics bay, **wired to the panel, not to the data bus**.
That is deliberate: the instrument that watches what breaks the terminals
cannot be behind a terminal. It reports flux in particles per
square centimetre per second, and it is the only warning of an event this
vessel has. Nothing else on board mentions the weather.

SEU HAZARD annunciates above 100 p/cm²·s. Like every caution on this ship it
reports a *measurement*, not a prediction: it means the environment is
hazardous now, not that anything has failed, and it will clear when the flux
falls whether or not something failed while it was lit.

## 10. What a high flux does, and what you can do about it

A single-event upset is a charged particle depositing enough energy in a
junction to flip a state. In a bus transceiver the ugly outcome is a
**latch-up**: a parasitic conduction path that switches on and stays on,
babbling across the medium and taking the whole bus with it (§2, FIM 42-11).

The defence follows from what a latch-up *is*:

**A parasitic path needs a rail. An unpowered board cannot latch up.**

That is the whole of it, and it is why the response to an elevated
environment is a breaker and not a repair. Opening a terminal's feed during an
event removes that terminal from the hazard entirely — and from the bus,
which is the price. You are choosing a known, reversible degradation over an
unknown, timed one. SOM 42-30-02 works the trade in order.

Two honest limits on all of this:

- **Protection is not prevention for the ship.** A terminal you shed is safe;
  the terminals you keep are exactly as exposed as they were. There is no
  configuration of this vessel in which an event costs nothing.
- **The monitor cannot tell you that you were right.** Ride out an event with
  everything powered and nothing may happen; shed everything and you will
  never know what you avoided. The reading is the only evidence either way,
  and the flight log is where it belongs.
